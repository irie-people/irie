# The charters

This skill holds Irie's verified copies of thirty-eight texts of rights, each in a file beside this one, the oldest first:

- **magna-carta.md**: Magna Carta, the charter King John granted at Runnymede in 1215, in William Sharp McKechnie's translation as a 1915 edition printed it: its preamble and 63 chapters.
- **magna-carta-1297.md**: Magna Carta as Edward I confirmed it in 1297, the charter on the English statute book with 37 chapters, in the statute book's old translation as Ruffhead's Statutes at Large (1763) prints it; its chapter 29 is the one the Petition of Right recites.
- **mayflower-compact.md**: the Mayflower Compact of November 11, 1620, the settlers' covenant and its forty-one signers, in Thorpe's 1909 print.
- **petition-of-right.md**: the Petition of Right of 1628, 3 Cha. I c. 1, as enacted, in the Statutes of the Realm's spelling, which recites Magna Carta and names "due p[ro]cesse of Lawe".
- **body-of-liberties.md**: the Massachusetts Body of Liberties of 1641, the Bay Colony's first code, its 98 liberties in Gray's edition of 1843, in the original spelling.
- **habeas-corpus-act.md**: the Habeas Corpus Act of 1679, 31 Cha. II c. 2, as the Statutes of the Realm prints it.
- **english-bill-of-rights.md**: the English Bill of Rights of 1689, "An Act declareing the Rights and Liberties of the Subject and Setleing the Succession of the Crowne", 1 Will. & Mar. sess. 2 c. 2, as enacted, in the Statutes of the Realm's spelling.
- **freedom-of-the-press-act-1766.md**: Sweden's Freedom of the Press Act of 2 December 1766, the first law to abolish censorship and open the state's papers, in Ian Giles and Peter Graves's translation (2016, CC BY 4.0).
- **virginia-declaration-of-rights.md**: the Virginia Declaration of Rights of June 12, 1776, in the National Archives' modernized rendering: its spelling and punctuation are today's, not 1776's, and verification.md says where it differs from the adopted text.
- **declaration.md**: the Declaration of Independence, with its 56 signers.
- **pennsylvania-declaration-of-rights.md**: the Pennsylvania Declaration of Rights of September 28, 1776, sixteen articles, as Thorpe's government print of 1909 gives it.
- **articles-of-confederation.md**: the Articles of Confederation, agreed in 1777 and ratified in 1781, their thirteen articles.
- **massachusetts-declaration-of-rights.md**: the Massachusetts Declaration of Rights of 1780, John Adams's thirty articles, as the Commonwealth's own text gives them, in its later spelling.
- **virginia-statute-for-religious-freedom.md**: Jefferson's Virginia Statute for Religious Freedom as passed on January 16, 1786, from Hening's Statutes at Large of Virginia.
- **northwest-ordinance.md**: the Northwest Ordinance of July 13, 1787, its fourteen sections and six articles of compact, in the National Archives' transcription.
- **constitution.md**: the Constitution of the United States, its signers, and all 27 amendments.
- **declaration-of-the-rights-of-man.md**: France's Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen of 26 August 1789, in Thomas Paine's translation as an 1894 edition prints it.
- **bill-of-rights.md**: the Bill of Rights, the first ten amendments, with the preamble of the 1789 joint resolution that proposed them, the two articles not ratified in 1791 and the attestation.
- **declaration-of-sentiments.md**: the Declaration of Sentiments of the Seneca Falls convention, July 1848, with its resolutions and signers.
- **emancipation-proclamation.md**: the Emancipation Proclamation of January 1, 1863, in the National Archives' transcription.
- **gettysburg-address.md**: the Gettysburg Address, November 19, 1863, the Bliss copy.
- **charter-of-the-united-nations.md**: the Charter of the United Nations of 26 June 1945, as amended, its preamble and 111 articles, in the UN's text; the Statute of the Court annexed to it is left out.
- **universal-declaration-of-human-rights.md**: the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, proclaimed on 10 December 1948: its preamble and 30 articles, following the resolution's official record.
- **european-convention-on-human-rights.md**: the European Convention on Human Rights of 4 November 1950, as amended, with the seven Protocols the Court's edition carries, in the Court's English text.
- **convention-relating-to-the-status-of-refugees.md**: the Convention relating to the Status of Refugees of 28 July 1951, its preamble and 46 articles, in OHCHR's text; the Schedule is left out.
- **civil-rights-act.md**: the Civil Rights Act of 1964 as enacted, its eleven titles, from the Statutes at Large.
- **voting-rights-act.md**: the Voting Rights Act of 1965 as enacted, from the Statutes at Large.
- **convention-on-racial-discrimination.md**: the International Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Racial Discrimination of 21 December 1965, its preamble and 25 articles, the UN's text following the certified true copy.
- **freedom-of-information-act.md**: the Freedom of Information Act, 5 U.S.C. § 552, as the United States Code sets it out today, with its source credit, the laws that made and amended it.
- **international-covenant-on-civil-and-political-rights.md**: the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights of 16 December 1966, its preamble and 53 articles, the UN's text following the certified true copy.
- **international-covenant-on-economic-social-and-cultural-rights.md**: the International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights, adopted the same day, its preamble and 31 articles, likewise.
- **privacy-act.md**: the Privacy Act of 1974, 5 U.S.C. § 552a, as the Code sets it out, with its source credit.
- **convention-on-discrimination-against-women.md**: the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women of 18 December 1979, its preamble and 30 articles, likewise.
- **convention-on-the-rights-of-the-child.md**: the Convention on the Rights of the Child of 20 November 1989, its preamble and 54 articles, in OHCHR's text.
- **americans-with-disabilities-act.md**: the Americans with Disabilities Act of 1990, its five titles.
- **freedom-of-information-act-2000.md**: the United Kingdom's Freedom of Information Act 2000, as enacted, whole, with its eight Schedules.
- **right-to-information-act.md**: India's Right to Information Act, 2005, as India Code sets it out, as amended.
- **convention-on-the-rights-of-persons-with-disabilities.md**: the Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities of 13 December 2006, its preamble and 50 articles, in the UN's text; the Optional Protocol is left out.

verification.md says where each text comes from, what it was checked against, the four marks of the Constitution and the Bill of Rights settled from the parchments themselves, the readings still unsettled in the other texts, and each file's SHA-512 fingerprint.

## Quote from the files, never from memory

- **Read the file and copy the words exactly.** The spelling, capitals and punctuation are the sources': "chuse", "Independance", "Brittish", "the Ratification of the Conventions of nine States", the 1689 Act's "cruell and unusuall Punishments" and "excessive Baile", the Articles' "Stile". Don't modernize them unless asked, and say so when you do.
- **Say whose English it is.** Magna Carta's words are McKechnie's translation of the Latin for 1215 and the statute book's old translation for 1297; the Rights of Man is Paine's translation and Sweden's Act Giles and Graves's; the Virginia Declaration is the Archives' modernized text; the Universal Declaration follows the 1948 official record where the UN's web text differs; the Gettysburg Address is the Bliss copy, one of five in Lincoln's hand. Say so when you quote them as the original's words.
- **Lines in italics are editorial.** Dates, notes and the amendments' headings are not the documents' words, so never quote them as the document. The headings of the Petition of Right, the Habeas Corpus Act and the English Bill of Rights are the Statutes of the Realm's marginal titles, and Magna Carta (1297)'s chapter titles are Ruffhead's, not the texts' words.
- **Cite by the documents' own parts.** Use "Article I, Section 8" (the parchment writes "Article. I." and "Section. 8."), "Amendment XIV, Section 1", the Declaration's paragraph, "Magna Carta, chapter 39", "the Petition of Right, section IV", "Virginia Declaration of Rights, Section 8", "the Northwest Ordinance, Article 6", "Universal Declaration, Article 19", "5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(6)", "5 U.S.C. § 552a(b)(2)" or "the Right to Information Act, section 3". The Code's paragraphs each start with their letter or number, so trace a citation down from its subsection: (b), then (6).
- **Say when words can't be found.** When a quotation isn't in the file word for word, say so and give the closest passage. "Life, Liberty and the pursuit of Happiness" is in the Declaration, not the Constitution.
- **Say when a clause was changed.** The Constitution's articles stand as written in 1787, including clauses later amendments changed. The notes under each amendment say which part it changed. When you quote a changed clause, say it was changed and by which amendment.

## How they bear on one another

Irie keeps links between the texts, each from a later text back to an earlier one, with a sentence saying what the one took from the other and quoting both; Irie's tests check every quotation against the texts. `irie ctl charters` lists them, and `--json` gives each one's sentence. In short:

- Magna Carta (1297) follows the 1215 charter, cut to 37 chapters. The Petition of Right drew on both, reciting chapter 29, and the Body of Liberties on the 1297 charter. The Virginia Declaration drew on Magna Carta (the law of the land, Section 8) and on the English Bill of Rights (bail, fines and punishments, Section 9).
- The Declaration of Independence drew on the Virginia Declaration ("created equal", "unalienable Rights"), and so did Pennsylvania's and Massachusetts's declarations, Massachusetts's on the English Bill of Rights too and following the Body of Liberties. The Rights of Man drew on Virginia's and Jefferson's, and the Universal Declaration on the Rights of Man.
- The Virginia Statute drew on the Virginia Declaration (the free exercise of religion), and the First Amendment on the Statute.
- The Articles of Confederation follow the Declaration. The Northwest Ordinance follows the Articles, as an act of their Congress, and drew on the Habeas Corpus Act. The Constitution follows the Declaration, which it dates itself from, replaced the Articles, and drew on the Habeas Corpus Act (Article I, Section 9) and the Northwest Ordinance (the Thirteenth Amendment's words).
- The Bill of Rights amends the Constitution and drew on Magna Carta in both charters (due process), the Petition of Right (due process, quartering), the Body of Liberties (torture, double jeopardy, cruel punishments), the English Bill of Rights (the Eighth Amendment's words, petition, arms), the Virginia, Pennsylvania (speech, press, arms) and Massachusetts (searches and seizures, arms) declarations, the Virginia Statute and the Northwest Ordinance.
- The Declaration of Sentiments drew on the Declaration of Independence, whose words it rewrites for women. The Emancipation Proclamation follows the Constitution (the Commander in Chief's power). The Gettysburg Address follows the Declaration, which it counts from, and the Proclamation.
- The Universal Declaration follows the UN Charter, whose peoples reaffirmed their faith in fundamental human rights, and drew on the Declaration of Independence, the Rights of Man and the Bill of Rights; the European Convention, both Covenants, the Conventions against racial and sex discrimination and the Convention on the Rights of the Child drew on the Universal Declaration, the Covenants standing beside each other, and the women's Convention follows the Declaration of Sentiments. The Civil Rights Act follows the Constitution (the Fourteenth Amendment) and the Universal Declaration, and the racial discrimination Convention follows it; the Voting Rights Act follows the Constitution (the Fifteenth) and the Civil Rights Act; the Disabilities Act follows the Civil Rights Act.
- The Refugee Convention drew on the UN Charter and the Universal Declaration (the right to seek asylum), and the Covenant on Civil and Political Rights follows it (the expulsion of aliens). The Disabilities Convention drew on the Universal Declaration, both Covenants, the Conventions against racial and sex discrimination (whose definitions of discrimination its own follows) and the Convention on the Rights of the Child, and follows the Americans with Disabilities Act (reasonable accommodation).
- The Massachusetts Declaration of 1780 follows the Mayflower Compact: its "social compact, by which the whole people covenants with each citizen" is what the settlers did when they did "covenant and combine ourselves together into a civil Body Politick".
- The Freedom of Information Act follows the Constitution, as an Act of Congress, Sweden's Press Act of 1766, the first such law, and the Universal Declaration (Article 19); the Privacy Act stands beside FOIA in the Code and follows the Constitution and the Universal Declaration (Article 12). The United Kingdom's Act follows FOIA and the Covenant; India's follows the Universal Declaration, the Covenant and FOIA.

The links are Irie's reading of the texts, not the documents' words: give one as Irie's link, never as a source.

## Check a copy

To check whether another copy is this one, compare its SHA-512 with verification.md's: `shasum -a 512 FILE`. To check a quotation, search the files for its words.

## On the local chain

Irie keeps these texts on a chain of its own on this computer, the Charters chain, in `$XDG_STATE_HOME/irie/chain/charters` (`~/.local/state/irie/chain/charters` unless that is set). Each version holds every text word for word, with its SHA-512, its date and its kind, and the links, and names the version before it by its own SHA-512, so the newest names the whole history. Together the texts and the links are a graph: the texts its nodes, the links its edges. A version is added only when a text or a link changes. `irie ctl charters` shows the newest version and checks every one: each version against its name, each text against its fingerprint, and each link against the texts. `irie ctl charters log` lists them, and `irie ctl charters export` prints every version's bytes, oldest first, one a line, for another tool to read the chain whole. The first build kept the chain as `chain/founding`, holding the four first texts in the form `irie.founding/1`; Irie moves it into the Charters chain and goes on from its newest version, so the history stays one. Nothing on it leaves this computer.

## In Irie

The Charters plugin shows the thirty-eight in the Charters tab of the Mac app's right sidebar, each with the links that say how it bears on the others. Its search finds a passage whatever its spelling or capitals, and its foot shows the chain's newest version.
