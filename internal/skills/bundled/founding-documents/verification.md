# How these copies were verified

Checked on 21 November 2026. The three founding documents are the National Archives' transcriptions, and the Freedom of Information Act is the United States Code's text, each set in plain Markdown. Each was then checked against every other copy at hand: word for word, and for the Bill of Rights, the Constitution's articles and the Freedom of Information Act, mark for mark. In four marks the Archives' transcription differs from its own parchments, and there these copies follow the parchments ("Settled from the parchments", below). Later that day nineteen texts joined them, the ones those four grew from or into, each from the body that keeps it, or from the cleanest public transcription where no such body gives it whole, and checked the same way: from Magna Carta in both its charters, the Petition of Right and the Habeas Corpus Act, through the Virginia texts, the Articles, the Northwest Ordinance, the Declaration of Sentiments, the Emancipation Proclamation and the Gettysburg Address, to the Universal Declaration, the Covenant, the Privacy Act and three countries' right-to-information laws.

Only the markup and the lines in italics are ours. The Code's footnotes, one in § 552 at subsection (a)(3)(E) and two in § 552a marked at (a)(1), (a)(8)(B)(x) and (u)(4)(A), are notes in italics after their paragraphs. The Archives marks two passages in Amendments XII and XIV that later amendments changed, with brackets and an asterisk. Here each mark is a note in italics after its passage instead, and the passage's words are unchanged. The Statutes of the Realm's footnotes to the English Bill of Rights, two variant readings, a word interlined on the Roll and three passages annexed to the Act in a separate schedule, are notes the same way, and its marginal notes are the headings; the Act itself has none. Where an edition sets a word in italics, the 1915 edition's Latin and French terms or the Code's "Provided, That", the word is plain here, since italics mark only our notes. Non-breaking spaces are plain spaces. Everything else, words, spelling, capitals and punctuation, is the source's.

## What was found

**Magna Carta.** The translator is McKechnie, named by the 1915 edition's headnote; the translation and the edition are both long out of copyright. Wikisource's transcription of the edition had copied two errors of text recognition that the printed page does not have, "one measure of com" for "corn" (chapter 35) and "clergy and lajonen" for "laymen" (chapter 62); each was checked against the page image and corrected, so the file reads as the page does. Against the Internet Archive's text recognition of the same scan, 4,920 words here against 4,941, every one of the 48 differences in words and 58 with marks is the recognition's: "oiu" for "our", "WiUiam", the running head "Appendix", a line-end hyphen, and one stop where the page reads on ("by our father, King Henry"). The Avalon Project's copy is McKechnie's translation altered throughout, "forever", "towards", "to wit", "enroll", with slips of its own and passages left out, so it is not a copy of this text, though it agrees with the printed page where Wikisource erred. legislation.gov.uk carries only the chapters of the 1297 charter still in force, in another translation: its chapter 29 is chapters 39 and 40 here.

**Magna Carta (1297).** The statute book's translation names no translator; Ruffhead's edition of 1763 reprints it, and Wikisource's validated transcription of that edition had 27 slips against the printed pages, each set right in the file and listed in the notes that made it: three change the sense, "shall not be taken but in the shires" (chapter 12), "she may honestly dwell" (chapter 7) and "or of any other" (chapter 31), one keyed a marginal phrase into chapter 27's text, which is a note here, and the rest are capitals and commas ("Difficulty", "Justices", "be holden"). The Internet Archive's recognition of the same scan agrees with the file wherever it reads at all. legislation.gov.uk's chapters in force, the same translation, agree word for word in the preamble and chapters 1, 9, 29 and 37. The edition's Latin column, running quotation marks, marginal citations of the law reports and notes on later statutes are left out; its notes on the translation are kept. The text dates Edward's confirmation in his twenty-eighth year, 1300, and the edition's footnote corrects it to 12 October 1297, which the chain takes.

**The Petition of Right.** Every word and every mark matches British History Online's copy of the Statutes of the Realm, headings included; BHO puts the two interlined words in brackets where these copies give a note. Every word matches legislation.gov.uk's revised text too, apart from the words that text leaves out as repealed (sections III to V, and a sentence of VIII), its expansion of the edition's contractions ("Comons" for "Co[m]mons", "present" for "p[re]sent"), five readings of its own ("Lieutenants" for "Lieuten'nts", "grievance" for "greivance", "Rightes" for "Rights"), and its reading of the Latin before the King's answer, below.
