# The charters

On 2026-11-21 the owner compared copies of the Constitution and the Bill of Rights, then asked for an official, verified copy in Irie's skills, and a plugin for the Bill of Rights and the Declaration of Independence. The owner added that a chain would start from it. Later that day they asked for the texts to be on a chain locally, and for the Freedom of Information Act too. Then: "extend all of the declaration of independance and bill of rights and rights to information act, i want you to put them all on the same chain, and i think i want to call it something meaningful", "or like same blockchain graph", and "bring in constitution and start on other official documents that are similar and adding to graph and connecting inferences". So the chain grew to ten texts, carries the links that say how each bears on the others, and is the Charters chain.

## What it is

- **A skill**, `/irie:founding-documents`. Its files hold thirty-eight texts word for word, the oldest first: Magna Carta (1215, in McKechnie's translation, and 1297, the statute book's), the Mayflower Compact (1620), the Petition of Right (1628), the Massachusetts Body of Liberties (1641), the Habeas Corpus Act (1679), the English Bill of Rights (1689), Sweden's Freedom of the Press Act (1766), the Virginia Declaration of Rights (1776), the Declaration of Independence, the Pennsylvania Declaration of Rights (1776), the Articles of Confederation, the Massachusetts Declaration of Rights (1780), the Virginia Statute for Religious Freedom (1786), the Northwest Ordinance (1787), the Constitution with all 27 amendments, the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen (1789, in Paine's translation), the Bill of Rights, the Declaration of Sentiments (1848), the Emancipation Proclamation and the Gettysburg Address (1863), the Charter of the United Nations (1945), the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948), the European Convention on Human Rights (1950), the Convention relating to the Status of Refugees (1951), the Civil Rights Act of 1964, the Voting Rights Act of 1965, the Convention against racial discrimination (1965), the Freedom of Information Act (5 U.S.C. § 552), the two International Covenants of 1966, the Privacy Act of 1974 (§ 552a), the Convention against discrimination against women (1979), the Convention on the Rights of the Child (1989), the Americans with Disabilities Act (1990), the United Kingdom's Freedom of Information Act 2000, India's Right to Information Act, 2005 and the Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (2006). Agents quote from these files, never from memory, and say so when a quotation isn't there.
- **A plugin**, Charters, with one part. It brings the skill and the Charters tab in the Mac app's right sidebar, which shows the thirty-eight. Its search finds a passage whatever its spelling, so "choose" finds the parchment's "chuse" and "cruel and unusual" the 1689 Act's "cruell and unusuall". Under each text's title the tab lists the links: what it drew on, and which later texts drew on it, each a sentence away. The tab also shows each text's SHA-512 and how the local chain checked.
- **A graph.** Each link runs from a later text back to an earlier one it grew from, with one of five plain words for how (drew on, amends, replaced, beside, follows) and a sentence for why that quotes both texts. The texts are the graph's nodes and the links its edges, and the chain carries both.
- **An explorer**, the Charters Graph: the texts as a map, on a timeline by when and where they were made, with their parts, the rights and ideas they name and the links between them, and the words beside. Window › Charters Graph opens it, and so does the Charters tab's Explore button, at the text the tab shows; `irie ctl charters graph` gives its page, one file that loads nothing, for a browser.
- **A local chain** of the texts and links, the Charters chain, which the core keeps. `irie ctl charters` checks it and lists the links, `irie ctl charters log` lists its versions, and `irie ctl charters export` prints every version's bytes for another tool to read.
- **A record**, `verification.md`, beside the texts. It says what each text was checked against and what was found, and lists the words and marks that careful copies still disagree on.

## The texts

The National Archives' transcriptions are the founding documents' source: the Declaration's engrossed parchment, the Constitution as it was inscribed on parchment, the Bill of Rights' enrolled original, Amendments XI–XXVII, the Articles of Confederation, the Northwest Ordinance, the Emancipation Proclamation, and the Virginia Declaration of Rights, which the Archives gives in modern spelling and the file says so. The Freedom of Information Act and the Privacy Act come from the United States Code, in the Government Publishing Office's 2024 edition; Title 5 is positive law, so the Code's text is legal evidence of the law (1 U.S.C. § 204). India's Right to Information Act comes from India Code, the Government of India's repository of its Acts, which India's copyright law lets anyone reproduce together with commentary or other original matter, as the notes around it are. The Petition of Right, the English Bill of Rights and the Freedom of Information Act 2000 are legislation.gov.uk's "original (as enacted)" texts, under the Open Government Licence; the Habeas Corpus Act, which legislation.gov.uk has only as revised, is the Statutes of the Realm's text as British History Online keyed it, and Magna Carta (1297) the statute book's translation as Ruffhead's Statutes at Large of 1763 prints it. The Covenant is the UN's text following its certified true copy. Translations must be free to carry: Paine's of the Rights of Man (1791) and the statute book's of 1297 are public domain, and Giles and Graves's of Sweden's Act (2016) is under CC BY 4.0, credited in its file. The Virginia Statute comes from Hening's print of 1823, the Body of Liberties from Gray's edition of 1843, Pennsylvania's Declaration from Thorpe's government print of 1909 and Massachusetts's from the Commonwealth's own text; the Civil Rights and Voting Rights Acts are the Acts as enacted in the Statutes at Large, their words settled against the printed pages; and the Declaration of Sentiments and the Gettysburg Address, the Bliss copy, come from the public transcriptions that give them whole, since no government page does. Each transcription was set right against the printed pages or the manuscript's images where it had slipped, and the record says where. The Universal Declaration is the United Nations' English text, following the 1948 official record where the web text strays from it; UN documents issued under a document symbol are in the public domain (ST/AI/189/Add.9/Rev.2). Magna Carta is the 1215 charter in William Sharp McKechnie's translation of 1914, as a 1915 edition printed it, from Wikisource's transcription checked against the scan: the National Archives' own translation of the 1297 charter is under copyright, so it is not used. The words, spelling, capitals and punctuation are kept. Only the markup and the lines in italics are Irie's: headings, dates and notes.

Each text was then checked against every other copy at hand, word for word and, where the copies allow, mark for mark: the Avalon Project's copies, the Archives' own second copies, other government prints, Cornell's Code, the 1819 and 1821 prints as text recognition reads them, the UN's official record. Every difference found is in `verification.md`, and the readings still unsettled are in its table.

## The graph

The links are Irie's reading of the texts, each defensible from the texts themselves, and most of them quote both ends:

- Magna Carta (1297) follows the 1215 charter, cut to 37 chapters. The Petition of Right drew on both, reciting "the Statute called The great Charter of the Liberties of England" in the statute book's words, and the Body of Liberties on the 1297 charter ("unlesse it be by vertue or equitie of some expresse law of the Country"). The Bill of Rights drew on the Body of Liberties too: no torture to confess, no one "twise sentenced", no punishments "inhumane Barbarous or cruel". The Virginia Declaration drew on Magna Carta ("except by the law of the land or the judgment of his peers", Section 8, after chapter 39) and on the English Bill of Rights (Section 9 is the Act's "excessive Baile … cruell and unusuall Punishments" in Virginia's spelling).
- The Declaration of Independence drew on the Virginia Declaration ("all men are created equal" after "all men are by nature equally free and independent"), as Pennsylvania's ("born equally free and independent") and Massachusetts's ("born free and equal") declarations did; the First Amendment drew on Pennsylvania's "freedom of speech, and of writing, and publishing their sentiments" and the Fourth on Massachusetts's "secure from all unreasonable searches, and seizures". The Rights of Man drew on Virginia's and Jefferson's ("Men are born, and always continue, free and equal in respect of their rights"), and the Universal Declaration on the Rights of Man. The Virginia Statute drew on the Virginia Declaration's "free exercise of religion", and the First Amendment on the Statute's "no man shall be compelled to frequent or support any religious worship".
- The Articles of Confederation follow the Declaration. The Northwest Ordinance follows the Articles, ordained "by the United States in Congress assembled", and drew on the Habeas Corpus Act ("the benefits of the writ of habeas corpus"). The Constitution follows the Declaration, dating itself "of the Independance of the United States of America the Twelfth", replaced the Articles, whose Article XIII had made "the union shall be perpetual", drew on the Habeas Corpus Act ("The Privilege of the Writ of Habeas Corpus shall not be suspended") and, in the Thirteenth Amendment, on the Ordinance's Article 6, "There shall be neither slavery nor involuntary servitude".
- The Bill of Rights amends the Constitution and drew on Magna Carta (due process), the Petition of Right ("due p[ro]cesse of Lawe"; soldiers quartered in houses), the English Bill of Rights (the Eighth Amendment is its words; petition; arms), the Virginia Declaration and the Northwest Ordinance (habeas corpus, jury, cruel or unusual punishments).
- The Declaration of Sentiments drew on the Declaration of Independence ("all men and women are created equal"). The Emancipation Proclamation follows the Constitution ("Commander-in-Chief, of the Army and Navy"). The Gettysburg Address follows the Declaration ("Four score and seven years ago", "all men are created equal") and the Proclamation ("a new birth of freedom").
- The Universal Declaration drew on the Declaration of Independence ("born free and equal in dignity and rights") and the Bill of Rights (Articles 5, 9 to 11 and 18 to 20).
- The Universal Declaration follows the Charter of the United Nations, whose peoples "reaffirmed their faith in fundamental human rights", and sets out the rights the Charter named. The European Convention, both Covenants, the Conventions against racial discrimination and against discrimination against women, and the Convention on the Rights of the Child drew on the Universal Declaration, which their preambles name (the last recalls that "childhood is entitled to special care and assistance"); the Covenants stand beside each other, adopted together, the first making Article 19 treaty and the second the right to education; the European Convention and the first Covenant make Article 19's freedom to receive and impart information binding; the women's Convention follows Seneca Falls' "woman is man's equal". The Civil Rights Act follows the Fourteenth Amendment's equal protection and the Universal Declaration's "without distinction of any kind", and the racial discrimination Convention follows the Act's opening of "any place of public accommodation"; the Voting Rights Act follows the Fifteenth Amendment, which its title names, and the Civil Rights Act; the Disabilities Act follows the Civil Rights Act's Title VII in form. The Freedom of Information Act follows the Constitution, as an Act of Congress, Sweden's Press Act of 1766, whose "free access to all archives" was the first such law, and the Universal Declaration (Article 19's right "to seek, receive and impart information"); the Privacy Act stands beside FOIA in the Code and names it, and follows the Universal Declaration (Article 12's privacy). The United Kingdom's Act follows FOIA and the Covenant; India's Right to Information Act follows the Universal Declaration, the Covenant and FOIA ("all citizens shall have the right to information").
- The Refugee Convention drew on the Charter and the Universal Declaration ("the right to seek and to enjoy in other countries asylum from persecution"), and the Covenant on Civil and Political Rights follows its Article 32 on expulsion. The Disabilities Convention drew on the Universal Declaration, both Covenants, the Conventions against racial and sex discrimination, whose definitions of discrimination its own follows, and the Convention on the Rights of the Child, and follows the Americans with Disabilities Act, whose "reasonable accommodation" it carries into international law.
- The Massachusetts Declaration of 1780 follows the Mayflower Compact, the settlers' "civil Body Politick" become its "social compact".

`internal/founding/links.go` holds them. A quotation in a link's why, in curly double quotes, is in one of the two texts word for word: the tests check each, and that each link runs from a later text to an earlier one, by a known kind, once, and that every text is in the graph. Irie never copies another tool's code, so a graph viewer that reads the chain reads Irie's format; the export is for that.

## The explorer

On 2026-11-23 the owner asked for one "for indie and the constitution and bill of rights etc graph to explore that stuff": the Declaration of Independence, the Constitution, the Bill of Rights and the rest, as a graph to explore. The Charters Graph draws the same texts, the same links and nothing from elsewhere, and adds three kinds of tie, each of which can be checked against the texts.

- **Parts.** A text divides into our headings (the Constitution's articles and sections, the amendments, a convention's articles) and, where it has none, the paragraphs it numbers itself: Magna Carta's 63 chapters, the Body of Liberties' hundred liberties with the capital laws of the 94th, the Ordinance's sections and articles, the sections of the Voting Rights, Civil Rights and Disabilities Acts and of India's Act, Sweden's paragraphs, and the subsections of §§ 552 and 552a, taken in order, (a), (b), (c), since (i) is also a roman numeral one level down. There are 1,577 of them.
- **Rights and ideas.** Forty-two, in six families (speech, press and belief; courts and punishment; equality and dignity; life, liberty and the home; work, school and welfare; government by consent), each named in a passage where its words are: whole words in the document's own passages, never our headings or notes, in any spelling, as the search finds them, so "cruel and unusual" finds 1689's "cruell and unusuall". The words are ours, chosen from the texts to find the passages that grant, guard or name a right, and each idea lists phrases in which its words mean something else, so the Supremacy Clause's "Law of the Land" isn't due process, the States' "equal Suffrage in the Senate" isn't the vote, and the President's "Disability" isn't disability. The page shows the words that found each passage.
- **Quotations.** A link's quotations are in its two texts word for word, which the tests check; the explorer pins each link to the parts that hold them, so the Eighth Amendment leads to the English Bill of Rights' bail clause, the Body of Liberties' liberties 42, 45 and 46, the Ordinance's Article 2 and the Universal Declaration's Article 5.
- **The same words.** Parts of two texts with the very same words, folded as the search folds them, are tied, to the letter or spelling aside: the Bill of Rights' ten amendments and the Constitution's first ten, to the letter; the Bill of Rights' "Article the second", which Congress proposed in 1789 and the States ratified in 1992, and the Twenty-seventh Amendment; Virginia's Section 9 and the English Act's bail clause, in Virginia's spelling; and the two Covenants' shared Article 1 and closing articles.

The page lays the texts out on a timeline that gives long gaps less room than short ones, a lane for each place: England and the United Kingdom, the American colonies and states, the United States, Europe, the United Nations and India. The ideas lie below, each under the middle of the texts that name it. A pick lights what it bears on: a text its links and the ideas it names; a part, the passages its links quote, in pills by their texts; an idea, every text that names it, with a count, and the links between them, which shows how a right travelled. The card beside gives the words, with each link's sentence and its quotations, the rights named, and a tree of the parts, WAI-ARIA's tree view. Its search finds rights, texts, parts and passages in any spelling, and opens a passage with its words lit.

It moves as a Mac app's map does: a two-finger scroll pans, a pinch zooms about the pointer, a drag tracks the hand and a flick glides on, a double-click zooms in, the camera flies along van Wijk and Nuij's smooth zoom-and-pan path, and any touch stops it. A pick is a step of the page's address, so Back and Forward (⌘← and ⌘→, and in a browser ⌘[ and ⌘]) walk the picks and a reload keeps its place; the arrow keys step from a pick to what it is tied to, that way; `?` lists every key. The view and the open rows are kept for the window's life in sessionStorage. It honours Reduce Motion and Increase Contrast, and draws in Roots' Evergreen, light or dark.

- `internal/founding/graph.go` makes the graph, `ideas.go` holds the ideas and their words, and `page.go` puts the graph, as JSON, into the page (`internal/founding/graph/`: `index.html`, `graph.css`, `graph.js`, Irie's own, with no library), one file that loads nothing: its Content Security Policy lets it load nothing, and the window's web view blocks the network besides.
- The core writes the page to `$XDG_STATE_HOME/irie/charters/graph.html`, a folder of its own that the window reads and nothing else, when the founding op is asked for `graph`, and again only when a build makes another.
- The tests hold every tie to the texts: each idea to the words it lights and to passages it must and mustn't find, each quotation tie to its link's quotation, each part inside its parent, and the same words to the letter. `TestThePageRuns` drives the page in a headless Chromium, with no window, at a desktop's size and a phone's: a pick and its card, Back and Forward, a search in old spellings, a passage found, the arrow keys, a click, a pinch, a scroll, the tree, and a draw at every zoom, with no error thrown. It skips where no headless Chromium is found (`IRIE_TEST_CHROME` names one).

## Fingerprints and the local chain

Each text's file is pinned by its SHA-512. The hash covers the file's bytes, UTF-8 with a line feed ending each line, so anyone can check a copy with `shasum -a 512`. The tests fail when a file's SHA-512 isn't the one `verification.md` gives, so no text changes without its record changing with it.

The core keeps the texts on a chain of their own, the Charters chain, in `$XDG_STATE_HOME/irie/chain/charters`, on the same `internal/chain` that keeps zen's versions. It is the first piece of the local chain [local-chain.md](local-chain.md) plans for the sidebar.

- **A version** is canonical JSON (RFC 8785), of the form `irie.charters/2`, holding every text whole, each with its SHA-512 as `sha512:` and 128 hex digits, its date (`when`) and its kind (charter, declaration, articles, constitution, amendments or statute), and the links (`from`, `to`, `how`, `why`). It is named by the SHA-512 of its own bytes, and names the version before it the same way, so the newest names the whole history.
- **When.** The core adds a version when it starts and at each look, and only when a text or a link has changed. A new build whose texts and links are unchanged adds nothing.
- **Checking.** Each look reads every version back. The store checks each against its name, so a version changed on disk after it was written fails. The check also hashes each text against its own SHA-512, and sees that each link names two texts the version holds.
- **The first build's chain.** The first build kept the four first texts as `chain/founding`, in the form `irie.founding/1`, with no dates, kinds or links. The core moves that folder into `chain/charters` once, when the new folder has nothing yet, reads its versions as they are, and puts the next version on after them, so the history stays one and the first version's name still stands. A version of any other form fails the check.
- **Export.** `irie ctl charters export` prints every version's bytes, oldest first, one a line (canonical JSON has no line break), so another tool can read the chain whole and `shasum -a 512` of a line is its version's name.
- **Only public standards** shape a version, so any tool with a JSON canonicalizer and SHA-512 can check one without Irie.
- **Like the local chain**, it carries no tokens, no money and no identity, and nothing on it leaves the computer. Anchoring a version's name on a public chain would be the local chain's opt-in hand-off, made by another tool.

## The name

The owner asked for a meaningful name. The chain is **Charters**: the National Archives calls the Declaration, the Constitution and the Bill of Rights the Charters of Freedom, Magna Carta is the Great Charter, and a charter is a grant of rights set down to be kept, which is what every text on it is and what the chain does with them. Other names weighed, each from the texts: Posterity (the Preamble secures liberty "to ourselves and our Posterity", which is what a chain that keeps the texts unchanged is for), Unalienable (the Declaration's word for rights that can't be taken away, as a hash chain's texts can't be altered), Liberties (what every text secures) and Lineage (what the links trace). The name shows in the chain's folder and form, the command, the plugin's title and the tab; the plugin's config key stays `plugins.founding`, the skill stays `founding-documents`, and the Go package `founding`, so a config and an agent's habits go on working, and `irie ctl founding` still answers.

## How it is built

- The texts are the skill's own files (`internal/skills/bundled/founding-documents`), embedded by `bundled_founding.go`, so an agent and the tab read the same bytes.
- `internal/founding` holds the table of texts (`Documents`: id, file, short name, date, kind), the links (`links.go`), the parser that reads a file into headings, the document's words, notes and lists of names, the finder, and the chain (`chain.go`).
- The core's `founding` op gives the tab the documents, the links, their chain as it just checked it, and, with `find`, the passages that hold some words, with `log`, the chain's versions, or with `export`, every version's bytes. Anyone may ask, an agent too: the texts are public, and the op only reads them and keeps their chain.
- `-tags nofounding` leaves the plugin out, texts and all. A company's copy leaves it out with the other personal plugins.
- It is off by default: the documents are a person's own reading, and a skill that is on costs every session a line of context.

## Open questions

- **Four marks, settled.** The Archives' transcription and other careful copies differed on four points. The Archives' high-resolution scans of the parchments settled each one, against the transcription: "Enumeration" (Art. I §9), "proper;" (Art. II §2), "another State;" (Art. III §2) and "In suits" (Amendment VII). The texts follow the parchments, and `verification.md` says how each reads. The chain kept the texts as first recorded, then the corrected ones as the next version.
- **Readings still unsettled.** `verification.md`'s table lists them: the English Bill of Rights' second declared right, where legislation.gov.uk's transcription repeats the first right's "Suspending of Laws" and the 1819 print, the revised text, Avalon and the marginal note all read "Dispensing with", and its "congizable"; the Virginia Declaration's "of twelve men" and "nor being descendible", the Archives' own words against the adopted text; the Articles' "con-firmed"; the Universal Declaration's comma. The files follow their sources; the originals would settle each.
- **The Virginia Declaration in the original's spelling** would need a literal transcription of the 1776 text; the Archives gives a modern rendering, and Hening's 1821 print is only text recognition.
- **The 1297 Magna Carta**, the charter the Archives displays, has no public-domain translation online as a whole; the Statutes of the Realm (1810) prints one, as a scan.
- **The House's online Code** was down for maintenance on 21 November 2026. Once it's back, check §§ 552 and 552a against it, current through Public Law 119-142 (November 10, 2026). Cornell follows it and matched.
- **Readings of the second batch still unsettled.** The Habeas Corpus Act's "an Action of false Imprisonment", where two independent copies read "an Action or Actions"; British History Online's keying slips in it ("standingh", "seaeventy"); the Petition of Right's Latin before the King's answer; the Ordinance's "descent"; the Proclamation's "St. James Ascension" and the order of its closing; all kept as the sources have them, and the 1819 prints' pages or the manuscripts would settle them.
- **No government page gives the Gettysburg Address's Bliss copy whole**, nor the Declaration of Sentiments, so those two come from validated public transcriptions; a government transcription of either would be a better primary.
- **Readings of the third batch still unsettled.** Magna Carta (1297)'s date line (1300 in the text, 1297 in the edition's note); the Virginia Statute's four words where Hening's print and the enrolled bill differ; the Covenant's Article 42 marks where the certified copy and the 1966 record differ; the UK Act's closing quotation mark in Schedule 6 and its sixteen straight apostrophes; all kept as the sources have them.
- **Readings of the fourth batch still unsettled.** The Body of Liberties' "so suffer" where Whitmore reads "so differ"; Massachusetts's "Any every" and its later spelling; the Voting Rights Act's two marks that may be specks; the Civil Rights Act's "(l)"; all kept as the sources have them.
- **The Federalist, held out.** Every public text of the eighty-five essays, the Library of Congress's included, is one uncredited e-text family that names no edition, runs paragraphs together in some twenty essays, lacks a passage of No. 6 and the closing "PUBLIUS." of two essays, and mixes readings ("inefficiency" for McLean's "inefficacy"). A page-checked transcription of Dawson's edition (1863) or Ford's (1898), both out of copyright and scanned, would meet the standard; the e-text does not.
- **India's Fundamental Rights, held out.** Part III of the Constitution of India, with its Preamble, was fetched from the Legislative Department's text as on 1 May 2024 and checked against its 2022 edition, word for word. Its basis to be carried here rests on a reading of India's Copyright Act, 1957: the 1949 text as a Government work whose sixty-year term (section 28) ended in 2009, and the amending Acts as Acts of a Legislature reproducible with commentary (section 52(1)(q)(ii)); the Department's site asks that its material be reproduced after permission by mail. It waits on the owner's word, or on that permission.
- **The American Declaration of the Rights and Duties of Man, held out.** The Inter-American Commission publishes the 1948 Declaration (https://www.oas.org/en/iachr/mandate/Basics/declaration.asp), but the OAS General Secretariat's terms (https://www.oas.org/en/terms_conditions.asp) allow copying "for non-commercial purposes so long as the GS/OAS is cited" and forbid users to "resell, redistribute, or create derivative works absent the express written permission" of the Secretariat; a public repository redistributes. It waits on that permission or on a public-domain print, such as the United States delegation's report on the Bogotá conference (Department of State publication 3263, 1948), of which no text was found online.
- **Readings of the sixth batch still unsettled.** The Refugee Convention's four words where OHCHR's text and the Treaty Series' print differ ("practise", "purposes", "States", "judgement"), and the Disabilities Convention's words where the UN's PDF and OHCHR's web page differ; the certified true copies would settle them.
- **More documents.** The same skill, tab, chain and graph would take the American and African regional charters of human rights (their terms to check), and the states' constitutions' declarations of rights after Virginia's, Pennsylvania's and Massachusetts's, each with its links.
