# Common Schools

On 2026-11-25 the owner asked for "another graph project about education and public schools", "similar to indie" (the Charters Graph, [founding-documents.md](founding-documents.md)), to "start with the establishment of public schools in the united states". On the look of it: "start with progressive disclosure, start with a pie chart, and then user clicks into stuff with breakdowns that turn into more pie charts". The same day, from other cards, they asked to add those cards' research: on who builds and keeps up the schools ("i want to add to the school graph and info"), on Blue Bird, the bus maker ("add to school graph"), and on church and state in public schools ("add it to the school graph"). Asked whether Irie's own words might name the laws about prayer and the Ten Commandments, and whether the page should go on the web as the charters' does, they answered "yes and yes".

Common Schools is that project. It takes its name from what Americans called their first public schools, schools common to every child of a town, and the movement that spread them.

## What it is

- **Texts, kept as the charters are.** Each is word for word from the body or print that keeps it, checked against at least one other copy, every difference settled on the print's page images and recorded in `verification.md` beside the texts, with each file's SHA-512. They are a skill's files, `/irie:common-schools`, so agents quote them from the files, never from memory.
  - **How public schools began:** Massachusetts Bay's school laws of 1642 and 1647, in the Records' own spelling; the school provisions of the first state constitutions (Pennsylvania and North Carolina, 1776; Georgia and Vermont, 1777; Massachusetts, 1780; New Hampshire, 1784); the Land Ordinance of 1785, which reserved "the lot N 16, of every township, for the maintenance of public schools"; the Ohio Company's terms of 1787; the Ohio Enabling Act of 1802, which offered the first new state "the section, number sixteen, in every township"; and Massachusetts's school attendance act of 1852. The Northwest Ordinance's "schools and the means of education shall forever be encouraged" is the charters' own copy, read from their files.
  - **Church and state**, from the separation-of-church-and-state card, which fetched and checked them the same way: Jefferson's letter of 1802 to the Danbury Baptists, the Equal Access Act, the Elementary and Secondary Education Act's section on school prayer, and the Ten Commandments laws of Louisiana (2024), Arkansas and Texas (2025) and Alabama (2026). The charters' Mayflower Compact, Declaration, Virginia Statute, Constitution and Bill of Rights, which those texts quote, are read from the charters' files.
- **Figures, each from a public source** named on the card beside it, read there on the day.
- **Links.** As in the charters, a link runs from a later text back to an earlier one, with one of the same five words for how (drew on, amends, replaced, beside, follows) and a sentence for why that quotes the texts in curly double quotes; the tests check that each quotation is in one of its two texts word for word. North Carolina's and Vermont's clauses of 1776 and 1777 take Pennsylvania's nearly word for word, New Hampshire's of 1784 Massachusetts's of 1780; Texas's poster is Louisiana's word for word.
- **An explorer**, Window › Common Schools: pie charts that open into smaller ones.

## The explorer

The page starts with one pie and nothing else to read but its slices. A click on a slice, or Return on its row, opens it: the slice grows to fill the circle and splits into its own parts, another pie. The trail above says where you are and takes you back up; Escape goes up a step, and Back and Forward walk the steps. Where a slice has no parts worth a pie, its card says what it is and gives the texts behind it; a text opens to its words, with its passages lit, its links to the other texts, and its fingerprint. A card can also open a pie beside its own, "another way to count it".

It has four starting pies, chosen above the pie, and opens on the first:

- **How they began.** The 48 states of 1922 by the land Congress gave them for their schools: the thirteen original states and five admitted later received none, Tennessee set its own aside, twelve states received section 16 of every township, fourteen sections 16 and 36, and three sections 2, 16, 32 and 36. The land groups open into the Census Bureau's divisions, where a group holds more states than a pie has room for, and each division into its states, by the acres of school sections they received; each state opens into the lands it could use for schools, by kind. The original states, and the five admitted later, open into their divisions, whose cards give the laws each state made.
- **The buildings.** What the school buildings need a year, $242 billion: what districts spend on building and on upkeep, and what each falls short. Building opens into who pays for it; upkeep's card opens the energy service companies' markets, of which schools are the largest, and the districts the GAO found needing HVAC work.
- **The buses.** The full-size school buses registered new in a year, Blue Bird's against the rest; Blue Bird opens into what its buses ran on, and into its sales. The card also opens who owns the buses, districts or contractors, and the largest contractors.
- **Church and state.** The Supreme Court's 22 decisions on religion in public schools, 1943–2025, by what each did: ended religion in a school's own program, protected a person's own faith or speech, public money and religious schools, and an even split; each group opens by decade into its decisions. The card also opens where the four states' Ten Commandments laws stood, Alabama's 2026 vote on student-led prayer, and the states in the 2027 scholarship credit. Irie's words about these laws are plain and legal; the religious words of prayers and commandments appear only inside the texts, quoted word for word.

A pie has at most six slices. Where a slice has more parts than that and no grouping of its own, such as the divisions or the decades, its pie shows the five largest and one more slice for the rest, which opens into them, so every part is a click away and no pie needs a seventh colour. Beside each pie a table gives every slice's name, amount and share, so no slice is known by its colour alone and every value can be read without the pie; the pie names a slice on its edge where there's room. A hover or a focus on either lights the slice and its row together.

**Colours.** The slices take Irie's six agent hues, worked out from the design library's numbers as the cards' are (Roots, `agents`: six hues 50° apart round the OKLCH wheel, leaving the needs red 30° clear on each side, at chroma 0.085), in an order that sets each slice's neighbours at least 100° round the wheel from it and alternates two lightnesses, so slices that touch differ in hue and in lightness. In light mode the lightnesses are 0.77 and 0.68 and in dark 0.69 and 0.60. Checked with the dataviz method's validator (adjacent pairs, the first slice beside the last): the worst neighbours stay 15.8 apart (ΔE in OKLab ×100) in normal vision and 8.8 under protanopia and deuteranopia, both over the floors, and `colour_test.go` holds them there. The chroma stays under that method's 0.10 floor on purpose: the owner's rule is a calm view, never neon, and the names on the slices and in the table carry what a stronger colour would. The slice for the rest is grey, the page's muted ink mixed into its ground. The page's own colours are Roots' Evergreen, light or dark, as the Charters Graph's are, and `tools/design.sh` keeps its copy the library's.

## How it is built

- The texts are the `common-schools` skill's files (`internal/skills/bundled/common-schools`), bundled with the charters' (`bundled_founding.go`), so an agent and the page read the same bytes. `internal/schools` holds the table of texts (`Documents`), the links, the figures with their sources (`figures.go`, `today.go`, `church.go`), the trees of slices the page draws (`tree.go`), the slices' colours (`colour.go`), and the page (`page.go`, with `internal/schools/pies/`: `index.html`, `pies.css`, `pies.js`, Irie's own, with no library). It reads files with `founding.Parse`, so a text here is written as a charter is.
- Common Schools is the Charters plugin's second part: the two share their way of keeping texts, the plugin's switch and its build tag, so `-tags nofounding` leaves both out. The founding op, asked for `schools`, writes the page to `$XDG_STATE_HOME/irie/schools/pies.html`, a folder of its own, and says where it is; `irie ctl schools` prints that path. Window › Common Schools shows the page in a web view that reads that folder alone and loads nothing from the network, as the Charters Graph's window does, and the Plugins menu's Common Schools part opens it.
- The page is one file that loads nothing: its Content Security Policy lets it load nothing, and it carries its data as JSON.
- **On the web**, at the owner's word: the site's copy, `site/schools/index.html`, and the page's own site, irie-people/common-schools. `go run ./tools/schools [DIR...]` writes the site's copy and the own site's into each folder named; `TestTheSiteHasThePage` fails while the site's is stale.
- The tests hold the texts to their fingerprints in `verification.md` and every passage the page lights to its text; every quotation in a link to its texts; every figure to its source's totals (Swift's Table 3 sums and his printed totals, each state's Table 5 row to its total, the report's spending and gaps to its need, the energy service companies' markets to their total, Blue Bird's buses by fuel to its sales, Alabama's votes to its certified share); every pie to its parts (no pie of one slice, no more slices than colours); the slices' colours to the floors above; the page to loading nothing; and drive the page in a headless Chromium at a desktop's size and a phone's (`TestThePageRuns`).

## The figures

- **Swift's tables.** The Bureau of Education's Bulletin, 1922, No. 47, Fletcher Harper Swift's Federal Aid to Public Schools (Government Printing Office, 1923): Table 2 names the eighteen states that received no federal land grants for common schools, "the thirteen original States and five admitted later"; Table 3 groups the twenty-nine that received township school sections, with the acres of each; Table 5 gives every state's lands available for schools, by kind. Tennessee, which received public lands in 1806 and set aside about 24,000 acres of them for schools by its own act, is in neither (page 9, Table 5's note 3). Alaska's sections 16 and 36 were reserved, not granted, in 1922, and Hawaii was a territory; Swift's tables leave both out, and so does the pie of 1922.
- **Two misprints, settled.** Table 3 prints Illinois's school sections as 966,320 acres; Table 5 prints 996,320, and only 996,320 makes Table 3's own total of 73,155,075 acres and Table 5's row for Illinois add up. Page 11 says Illinois received 121,629 acres of salt lands; Table 5 prints 121,029, which alone makes the column's total of 606,045. The page uses Table 5's figures, and the tests hold every total.
- **The divisions** are the Census Bureau's nine, as its map of regions and divisions lists them. A division with one state of a group shows as the state itself.
- **The buildings**: the State of Our Schools 2025 report (21st Century School Fund, National Council on School Facilities, International WELL Building Institute), in 2024 dollars, building the yearly average over fiscal 2014–2023 and upkeep over 2019–2023, each against the report's standard of 4% and 3% of the buildings' replacement value. Its upkeep figures were corrected on 27 January 2026, to $74 billion spent and $29.3 billion short; the research had the first release's $70 and $34 billion. Who paid for building is the report's Chart 13, local 80%, state 17%, federal 3% (80.5%, about 16% and 3.2% in its text). The GAO's 41% of districts and about 36,000 schools needing HVAC work are a low estimate with a margin of 9,000. The energy service companies' markets are Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory's table for 2024, schools 30% of $10.69 billion.
- **The buses**: 31,020 new Type C and D buses registered in the US and Canada in the year to September 2025, by S&P Global Mobility's count in Blue Bird's investor presentation, against a 1985–2025 average of about 30,500. Blue Bird's 9,409 are its sales anywhere in the world, so its share, about 30%, is ours; its buses by fuel and its sales by segment are its annual report's. Buses by owner are School Bus Fleet's 2024–25 counts in the 31 states that report both; the largest contractors' are its 2026 list's yellow buses, Beacon Mobility's 14,650 (the research had the 2025 list's 12,000). The American School Bus Council's "more than 480,000 school buses" and "more than 25 million schoolchildren" are from its own page as archived in 2019: its domain has since passed to others, so only the archived copy is linked.
- **Church and state**: each decision's citation, date, vote and holding read in the United States Reports or the Court's own opinion; where each Ten Commandments law stood on 25 November 2026, from the courts' opinions, orders, calendar and docket (Louisiana's full Fifth Circuit divided 11 to 7, as the opinion's own lists give it, not 12 to 6; the Supreme Court has the Texas petition and Louisiana's own on its conference of 11 December 2026); Alabama's Amendment 3 as certified on 23 November 2026; the IRS's list of the states in the 2027 scholarship credit, as of 13 November 2026. Left out of the pies for want of a firm count: the Texas districts' votes on daily prayer periods (15 yes by one count, about 110 of 120 tracked no by another) and the states that must release students for religious classes off campus ("at least 12" in 2025, since changed in Kentucky and North Carolina). Living people's names stay out of Irie's words: the decisions are named as they are cited, and justices, reporters and executives are left out.

## Next

- **More texts.** For the land states, the acts that admitted them with their school sections, after Ohio's; for the original states, the colonial laws after Massachusetts Bay's (Connecticut's code of 1650) and the school laws of the common-school movement (New York's of 1812, Pennsylvania's Free School Act of 1834, Massachusetts's Board of Education of 1837); then Reconstruction's constitutions, which wrote schools into the South's. For the buildings and buses, the laws the owner's research names: New York's Wicks Law (1912) and Pennsylvania's Separations Act (1913), the Davis-Bacon Act (1931), Public Law 81-815 (1950), the Motor Vehicle and Schoolbus Safety Amendments of 1974, the CARES Act's § 18003 and the American Rescue Plan's § 2001, the Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act's Clean School Bus Program, and the state school-building authorities. For church and state, the Court's opinions themselves, Texas's SB 11 and Alabama's Amendment 3.
- **Alaska and Hawaii**, admitted in 1959, from the acts that admitted them.
- **A chain**, as the charters have: the texts, links and figures as versions named by their SHA-512.
- **Moving facts**: the Supreme Court's conference of 11 December 2026 on the Ten Commandments petitions, the Eighth Circuit's ruling on Arkansas's law, Texas's new attorney general on 1 January 2027 and the IRS's list of states; each card says the day its facts were read.
